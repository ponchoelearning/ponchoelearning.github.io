Scenario-based learning

How to Design eLearning for Decisions With No Perfect Answer

A practical guide to creating realistic scenarios that help employees weigh evidence, recognise trade-offs and make sound workplace decisions.

Some workplace decisions have one clear answer. A safety step must be followed. A required field must be completed. A specific person must approve the request.

Other decisions are less straightforward.

An employee may need to balance customer needs with company policy. A manager may need to protect a deadline while supporting a team member. A compliance officer may need to act with incomplete information.

In these situations, several responses may appear reasonable. The learner must decide which priority matters most and explain why.

This is where scenario-based eLearning becomes useful. A well-designed scenario does not ask learners to guess what the course writer wants. It gives them a safe place to examine evidence, consider consequences and practise making a defensible choice.

A useful rule: If experienced employees could reasonably discuss the answer, the activity should assess the quality of the learner’s reasoning, not only the option they select.

Start with a real decision, not a broad topic

“Improve leadership skills” is too broad for one activity.

“Respond when a capable employee repeatedly misses deadlines” gives the design team something more useful. It identifies a situation, a decision-maker and a performance problem.

When planning the course, speak with subject matter experts and people who perform the role. Ask where valid priorities come into conflict.

You may uncover decisions such as:

  • Resolve a complaint quickly or investigate it more fully.
  • Keep a project moving or pause to address a possible safety risk.
  • Apply a policy consistently or account for unusual circumstances.
  • Protect confidential information or share enough detail to obtain help.
  • Support an employee or address an ongoing performance problem.

These are useful learning moments because experienced employees may approach them differently. The goal is not always to teach one fixed answer. It may be to teach a reliable decision process.

The CDC’s guidance on assessing training needs recommends defining the expected learning outcome before choosing the solution. For a judgement task, the outcome should describe what a sound decision looks like.

For example, the learner may need to:

  • Gather important missing information.
  • Identify the main risk.
  • Consult the right person.
  • Apply agreed decision criteria.
  • Record and explain the decision.
  • Take an appropriate next step.

A clear outcome keeps the scenario focused on performance rather than entertainment.

Give the learner enough context to think

A realistic scenario needs context. However, realism does not mean adding every available detail.

Give learners the information that would influence the decision:

  • What has happened?
  • Who is involved or affected?
  • What information is known?
  • What information is missing?
  • What rules or goals apply?
  • What time pressure exists?
  • What could happen next?

Remove details that do not change the choice. Decorative complexity makes an activity harder to read without making it more valuable.

Imagine a customer service scenario. The learner receives a complaint from a long-term customer whose refund request falls outside the usual policy.

The learner may need to know:

  • How far outside the policy the request sits
  • Why the customer could not submit it sooner
  • Whether the employee can approve an exception
  • Whether similar exceptions have been made
  • What evidence the customer has provided

The customer’s favourite colour or unrelated purchase history does not help the learner decide.

Official GOV.UK exercise guidance also explains that realistic scenarios help participants test practical skills and understand the effects of their actions. It advises designers to let clear objectives drive the scenario.

Write options that a real person might choose

Weak scenarios often have one sensible option and three careless or absurd choices.

Learners can pass these activities by noticing the writing pattern. They do not need to apply workplace judgement.

Instead, build each option around a believable line of reasoning.

Consider a manager dealing with a missed deadline. The manager could:

  1. Address the issue immediately to protect the project.
  2. Ask what caused the delay and whether support is needed.
  3. Wait for another missed deadline to avoid overreacting.
  4. Move the work to another employee to reduce short-term risk.

Each option has an understandable motive. One protects the deadline. Another seeks context. A third avoids unnecessary conflict. The last reduces immediate delivery risk.

The options should not be equally strong, but they should all be plausible.

Ask subject matter experts whether they have seen people choose each response. If nobody would realistically select an option, rewrite it.

Define what makes a decision stronger

Before writing the feedback, agree on the criteria for a sound decision.

These criteria may come from:

  • Organisational policy
  • Professional standards
  • Safety requirements
  • Customer commitments
  • Established expert practice
  • The limits of the employee’s authority

Depending on the task, a strong response may:

  • Manage immediate risk.
  • Collect important missing information.
  • Stay within the employee’s authority.
  • Consider the people affected.
  • Follow the required process.
  • Create a clear record.
  • Include an appropriate escalation or follow-up.

These criteria give the design team a consistent way to compare responses. They also stop feedback from becoming “This is correct because the course says so.”

If two responses are both acceptable, say so. Explain why one may be stronger under the conditions shown.

Show believable consequences

Consequences help learners connect a choice with its likely effect.

They do not need to be dramatic. In fact, exaggerated consequences can make the learning less credible.

A poor customer response may lead to repeat contact or a manager escalation. It does not need to create an instant public crisis.

A weak coaching question may cause an employee to become guarded. It does not need to make them resign immediately.

Show consequences through:

  • A short follow-up scene
  • A message from another person
  • A change in the customer’s response
  • A project status update
  • A manager’s observation
  • A brief explanation from the course

The consequence should be proportionate and connected to the choice. Learners need to understand which part of their decision produced the result.

Explain the trade-off in the feedback

“Correct” and “incorrect” are often too blunt for judgement-based activities.

Useful feedback should:

  1. Recognise the learner’s likely intention.
  2. Identify what the response handles well.
  3. Explain the risk or information it overlooks.
  4. Suggest a stronger next step.

For example:

Your response protects the deadline, but it acts before you know why the work is late. Start with a short conversation to confirm the cause. You can then agree on a recovery plan with clear responsibilities and dates.

This feedback does not dismiss the learner’s reasoning. Protecting the deadline is a valid concern. The problem is acting before collecting essential information.

Compare that with:

Incorrect. You should speak with the employee first.

The second response identifies the preferred answer but teaches very little. It does not explain why the learner’s choice was weaker or how to improve it.

Let learners compare different approaches

Judgement improves when people can compare their reasoning with another approach.

After the learner responds, show how an experienced employee might handle the same situation. Explain what that person notices, what they prioritise and what they would do next.

Avoid presenting the expert as someone who always knows the answer immediately. Good decision-makers often pause, collect information and test their assumptions.

You can ask learners:

  • Which fact had the most influence on your choice?
  • What information would you seek before acting?
  • Which risk did you prioritise?
  • What might make another option more suitable?
  • What would you record or communicate after deciding?

These questions help learners examine how they reached the answer, not only whether they selected the preferred option.

Give learners another attempt

When a decision is complex, one attempt may not be enough.

After showing feedback, allow the learner to:

  • Revise the original choice.
  • Continue from the consequence.
  • Request more information.
  • Try a similar situation with one changed condition.
  • Compare two acceptable responses.

Changing one detail can be particularly useful.

A manager may choose one response when a deadline is flexible and another when a safety-critical task is already late. A customer service employee may handle a minor exception differently from one involving possible fraud.

These variations show learners that good judgement depends on context.

Do not hide uncertainty

Some courses remove uncertainty because stakeholders want every activity to have a clear correct answer.

That may make scoring easier, but it can distort the work.

If a real decision depends on missing information, allow the learner to request that information. If different policies could lead to different responses, name the assumption used in the activity.

You can also explain that several actions may be defensible if they meet the required criteria.

This does not mean accepting every response. Some choices will still create unnecessary risk, ignore important evidence or exceed the employee’s authority.

The aim is to be clear about why a response is stronger.

Assess reasoning as well as the final choice

A score can show whether someone selected the preferred option. It cannot always show why.

For important decisions, add a small prompt that asks the learner to explain their reasoning.

They might answer:

  • What was the main risk?
  • Which piece of information influenced your decision?
  • Who would you involve next?
  • What would you document?
  • How confident are you in this choice?

The response does not need to be an essay. One or two focused questions can reveal whether the learner noticed the important facts and applied the agreed criteria.

For higher-risk tasks, online learning may need to be followed by discussion, observation or supervised practice. A digital scenario can provide preparation and consistency, but it should not be expected to prove every aspect of workplace competence.

Review scenarios with the people who do the work

A scenario may look realistic to the project team but feel artificial to employees.

Before publishing, ask representative learners and subject matter experts to review it.

Check whether:

  • The situation could genuinely occur.
  • The learner has the information they would normally have.
  • The language sounds natural.
  • The options reflect believable behaviour.
  • The consequences are proportionate.
  • The feedback explains the decision clearly.
  • The preferred response fits the learner’s authority.

Pay attention when reviewers disagree. Their disagreement may reveal that the policy is unclear, expert practice varies or the scenario does not contain enough information.

That is useful project feedback. Training cannot resolve an organisational problem that has no agreed answer.

Design for better judgement at work

Decisions with no perfect answer are difficult because they involve competing priorities.

Good eLearning does not hide that tension. It makes the tension safe to explore.

Start with a real decision. Provide only the context that matters. Write believable options, show proportionate consequences and explain the trade-offs. Then give learners a chance to compare, reflect and try again.

The result is not a course that simply tells people what to think. It is a learning experience that helps them make a sound choice when the right answer is not obvious.
